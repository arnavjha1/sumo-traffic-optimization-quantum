Abstract- Urban traffic signal control is often dominated by fixed cycles and local decisions
that fail to account for fluctuations in demand and optimize network-wide delay. This study
develops and evaluates two novel contributions: Pressure-Based Energy Optimization and
a QAOA-Based Optimization System. The pressure-based energy optimization framework

pressure alignment and neighbor coupling are rewarded and switching is penalized. QAOA

QAOA circuit to sample complete network-wide signal configurations from the same Ising
Hamiltonian optimized by Classical Global, a classical version of the QAOA Optimization

1 Introduction
Urban traffic congestion is one of the most persistent and costly challenges facing modern cities.
In the United States, the 2025 Urban Mobility Report estimates that the average driver lost 63
hours to traffic congestion in 2024, with nationwide congestion costs reaching approximately $269
billion annually (1). Beyond economic damage, inefficient traffic flow elevates greenhouse gas
emissions and lowers air quality for urban residents. Cities and towns together now house 81%
of the world’s 8.2 billion people, with two-thirds of future population growth projected to occur
in cities through 2050 (2). As global urbanization continues to intensify, the need to develop
smarter traffic management has never been greater. At the heart of this problem lies signalized
intersections, which are major sources of delay and capacity constraint in urban road networks,
where signal timing directly influences vehicle delay (3).
For the purposes of this study, three primary failure modes are considered:starvation, where
high-demandapproachesreceiveinsufficientgreentime;overflow,wheredownstreamqueuesspill
backintoupstreamapproachesandwasteavailablegreentime;andcongestion,wherelongqueues
and repeated acceleration delays reduce vehicle discharge efficiency through the network.
Traditionalfixed-timesignalsystemsoperateonpredeterminedcyclelengthsderivedfromhistorical
patterns and remain widely used for their simplicity and predictability. However, the fundamental
limitation of fixed-time systems is that they use predetermined timing plans and therefore cannot
react directly to unanticipated real-time demand fluctuations. Although coordinated fixed-time
systems can synchronize offsets across intersections, their timing plans remain pre-established
rather than continuously optimized from observed traffic conditions. Adaptive traffic control
systems improve upon fixed-time signals by adjusting phase timing in response to real-time traffic
conditions such as queue lengths. Some systems use local vehicle detectors to modify signal
timing at individual intersections, while coordinated systems such as SCOOT coordinate multiple
intersectionsalongmajorroadwaystoimprovetrafficflowacrossalargernetwork(4). Max-pressure
control uses queue lengths to select signal phases that minimize the imbalance between upstream
and downstream vehicle queues at each intersection (3). Reinforcement learning approaches treat
signal control as a sequential decision-making problem, enabling intersections to learn signal
timing strategies that reduce congestion through repeated interaction with the traffic environment
(5). Theseadaptiveapproachessubstantiallyexpandbeyondstatictiming,buttheydifferinthescope
and mechanism of coordination they provide. Max-Pressure, PressLight, and MPLight primarily
generate decisions from local or immediately downstream traffic conditions, whereas systems

2.1.1 Experimental Design
Experiment 1: Saturated𝛼-Sweep
The first experiment uses 600-second saturated simulations to isolate the effect of vehicle turning
behavior on controller performance and measure how well controllers can perform under extreme
congestion in peak traffic conditions that would be present in urban cities such as Seattle. The
primary source of congestion and delay is from the peak hours of traffic rather than low traffic
conditions, so it is important to isolate each controller’s ability to adapt to these high traffic
conditions. Vehicle inflow is held at the maximum feasible injection rate, simulating the highest
level of traffic congestion for a given network topology. The probability that a vehicle continues
straight through an intersection,𝛼, is swept from 0.0 to 1.0 in increments of 0.1. The remaining
probability,1−𝛼,issplitequallybetweenleftandrightturns. Thesaturated𝛼-sweepisperformed
on both the2×2and3×3networks to evaluate whether controller behavior remains consistent
as network size and coordination complexity increase, since a controller that performs well on a
limitednetworktopologymightnotactuallyperformwellonanurbancitynetworktopology,which
is much larger and complex.

2.1.3 Simulation Pipeline
The experimental framework integrates three primary components: (1) a microscopic traffic sim-
ulator modeling individual vehicle movements, (2) a traffic state analyzer that computes real-time
queue lengths and pressure metrics at each intersection, and (3) signal control using the metrics to
execute optimization algorithms and issue phase change commands.
The Simulation of Urban Mobility (SUMO) platform, version 1.27.0, serves as the microscopic
simulator, interfaced through the Traffic Control Interface (TraCI) API for real-time state queries
and signal command injection. The data pipeline connecting Seattle Open Data to the simulation

2.4 Max-Pressure Controller

2.9.9 State Machine Logic
Each intersection maintains its active phase and the elapsed duration of that phase. SUMO
advances in one-second simulation steps, while new controller recommendations are generated
according to the decision cadence defined for each controller. Between decision epochs, the most
recent recommendation is retained. If the minimum-green requirement has been satisfied and the
recommendedphasediffersfromtheactivephase,thesignalenterstheprescribedyellowandall-red
transitionsequencebeforeactivatingtheopposinggreenphase. Otherwise,thecurrentgreenphase
is maintained.

3.9 Synthetic Quantum-Noise Sensitivity
Figures25and26evaluatetheresponseofthe3×3QAOAcontrollerat𝛼=0.7tothreesynthetic
perturbation mechanisms: Gaussian angle perturbation, depolarizing noise, and readout error.
Figure 25 reports downstream traffic performance and Figure 26 reports solution quality. Table 23
provides the corresponding means and confidence intervals. These experiments are intended as
controlled synthetic robustness tests and should not be interpreted as a device-specific model of
current quantum hardware.
Across all three noise mechanisms, downstream traffic metrics were relatively stable. The ideal
reference produced a mean travel time of 197.50 s, waiting time of 43.75 s, and throughput of
979.9 vehicles. Under angle perturbation, mean travel time ranged from 195.05 s to 200.35 s and
waiting time from 43.76 s to 44.23 s. Throughput showed somewhat greater variation, ranging
from 950.3 to 975.3 vehicles, but the broad confidence intervals substantially overlapped the
ideal reference. There was therefore no monotonic deterioration in traffic performance as angle
perturbation increased.
Depolarizing noise produced an even flatter traffic response. Across probabilities from 0.5% to
2%, mean travel time remained between 197.46 s and 198.03 s and waiting time between 43.80 s
and 44.05 s. Throughput ranged from 973.6 to 998.3 vehicles. Readout perturbation showed the
same general pattern: neither travel time nor waiting time deteriorated monotonically as the error
probabilityincreased,andthroughputremainedwithinacomparativelynarrowrangerelativetothe
variability of the closed-loop simulation.
Solution quality was more sensitive than traffic performance and, importantly, did not degrade
monotonically with synthetic noise. A small angle perturbation of 0.025 rad increased the mean
gap from the ideal value of 23.323 to 29.552 and reduced recovery from 41.42% to 34.87%.
70

=== PDF PAGE 71 ===
However, at 0.100 rad the mean gap decreased to 21.408 and recovery returned to 40.38%. Thus,
increasing angle perturbation did not produce a simple dose–response deterioration.
The depolarizing experiment produced an even more counterintuitive pattern. Mean optimality
gap decreased from 17.907 at 0.5% depolarizing probability to 11.983 at 2%, while optimum
recovery increased from 46.50% to 54.62%. Readout perturbation similarly reduced the mean gap
from 23.371 at 1% to 19.264 at 5%, while recovery increased from 40.80% to 45.22%. These
results do not imply that physical quantum noise improves optimization. Instead, within the
simplified synthetic perturbation model used here, the introduced stochasticity alters the sampled
configuration distribution and can occasionally move sampling toward configurations with lower
measured energy.
The principal robustness result is therefore not monotonic degradation but a separation between
optimization-level sensitivity and traffic-level sensitivity. Synthetic perturbations measurably
changed optimality gap and recovery probability, yet the resulting travel-time and waiting-time
distributions remained comparatively stable. This indicates that the closed-loop traffic controller
can tolerate low-to-moderate perturbations in QAOA solution quality under the tested conditions,
consistent with the earlier finding that exact Ising optimality is not a prerequisite for competitive
traffic performance on the3×3network. At the same time, because the noise mechanisms are
synthetic rather than calibrated to a specific quantum processor, these results establish algorithmic
robustness under controlled perturbations rather than hardware-level fault tolerance.

4 Discussion
4.1 Contribution 1: Pressure-Based Energy Optimization
4.1.1 Where Pressure-Based Energy Optimization Adds Value
The first contribution of this study is the Pressure-Based Energy Optimization framework. The
central question for this contribution is not whether Classical Local, Classical Global, or QAOA is
73

=== PDF PAGE 74 ===
the strongest implementation of the energy objective; rather, it is whether adding the structure of
theenergyformulationprovidesusefultraffic-controlbehaviorbeyondconventionalpressure-based
control,whichisusedincontrollerssuchasMax-Pressure. Forthisreason,Max-Pressureprovides
the most important conceptual benchmark. Both approaches respond to upstream and downstream
traffic conditions, but the proposed energy framework additionally incorporates phase-switching
cost and neighboring-intersection coupling into the decision objective. The three energy-based
controllers are therefore considered collectively in this section as implementations of the same
underlying contribution.
The clearest evidence for the value of this additional structure appears when traffic demand is high
and interactions between intersections become important. In the saturated2×2experiment, the
energycontrollersweregenerallycompetitivewithMax-Pressureacrosstheintermediateandhigher
portionsofthe𝛼-sweep,particularlyinthroughput. At𝛼=0.7,forexample,thethreeenergy-based
controllers completed 941, 928, and 942 vehicles, compared with 906 under Max-Pressure. Their
waiting times were also comparable to Max-Pressure, remaining within approximately 0.4 s of its
39.11 s value. These results indicate that once traffic is sufficiently dense for network-wide signal
decisions to affect more than the immediately visible queue, the additional structure of the energy
objective can provide additional benefit to network discharge without a corresponding increase in
stopped delay.
This distinction becomes stronger on the3×3network, showing that the energy optimization
is more specialized for higher complexity scenarios on more complex network topologies. At
𝛼=0.7, allthreeenergy-basedcontrollersexceededMax-Pressureinthroughput, completing944,
940, and 980 vehicles compared with 925. They also all reduced average waiting time relative to
Max-Pressure: 44.21 s, 43.54 s, and 43.75 s compared with 47.16 s. Similar behavior is visible
acrossmuchoftheintermediate𝛼rangeinFigures9–11. Theimportantresultisnotwhichenergy
implementation produced the lowest individual value, but that the family as a whole became more
competitive with Max-Pressure when the network expanded from four to nine intersections.
This scaling pattern provides the strongest support for the proposed contribution. Max-Pressure is
highlyeffectivewhenthecontrolproblemcanberepresentedprimarilybytheimmediateimbalance
between an upstream queue and its downstream receiving link, but it is much more limited under
higher network complexity, which is where energy optimization can really provide benefit. The
energy formulation retains pressure information but also penalizes unnecessary switching and
represents interactions between neighboring intersections. Those additional terms should matter
mostwhenvehiclesrepeatedlypropagatefromonecontrolledintersectionintoanotherandalocally
attractive phase decision can alter the traffic state encountered downstream. The stronger relative
performance of the energy controllers on the3×3network is consistent with exactly this type of
coordination becoming more important as the number of internal corridors and interacting queues
increases. Theenergyoptimizationcontrollersareonlyabletocouplewith2intersectionstypically
to make decisions on the2×2grid, but on this larger grid, there is a center with 4 coupled
intersections and edges with 3 intersections to couple each including the center, giving the energy
optimization an advantage due to additional coupling.
The reinforcement-learning and cycle-based benchmarks provide useful context without changing
74

=== PDF PAGE 75 ===
this central comparison. CoLight and MPLight achieved some of the strongest delay results
in the study, demonstrating that learned policies can also capture traffic interactions effectively.
SCOOTbecamesubstantiallymorecompetitiveinthe24-hourcasestudythanintheshortsaturated
experiment,showingthatlonger-horizoncoordinationcanalsoproducebenefitsthatarenotvisible
in a 600-second test. These results prevent the energy framework from being interpreted as the
only viable approach to coordinated control. Instead, its contribution is more specific: it provides
a single, interpretable objective that extends pressure-based control with switching stability and
explicit inter-intersection interaction, and this additional structure becomes most useful when the
network is sufficiently congested and interconnected for purely immediate pressure response to
become incomplete.
The Seattle case study reinforces this interpretation under time-varying rather than continuously
saturated demand. On the2×2network, Max-Pressure generally remained stronger than the
energy-based controllers during sparse periods. On the3×3network, however, this gap narrowed
and sometimes reversed during the more sustained daytime demand. During the AM commute,
the energy controllers included travel-time values of 76.62 s and 74.93 s, compared with 79.39 s
forMax-Pressure,whileduringmiddaytheyincludedvaluesof81.49sand82.03scomparedwith
89.33 s for Max-Pressure. The waiting-time comparison showed the same general shift, with an
energy-controller value of 24.95 s during midday compared with 28.37 s for Max-Pressure. Thus,
the benefit observed in the saturated experiment was not restricted to synthetic maximum-demand
conditions; it also appeared during periods of realistic urban demand once the network contained
enoughinteractingintersectionsforcoordinationtomatterandalsoenoughdemandforthelambda

4.2 Contribution 2: QAOA Global Optimization
4.2.1 What QAOA Adds Beyond Exact Classical Global Optimization
The second contribution must be separated from the first. Classical Global and QAOA optimize
the same network-wide Ising Hamiltonian and operate over the same global signal configuration
space. Exact Classical Global exhaustively enumerates that space and therefore provides the
true minimum-energy state at each decision epoch. Consequently, any difference between the
two controllers cannot be attributed to anything other than the quantum circuit and annealer.
The scientific question is narrower:what changes when exact deterministic minimization of the
Hamiltonian is replaced by QAOA’s stochastic selection of near-optimal global states?
On the2×2problem, the answer is initially that very little changes in the optimization itself.

0.98 s [95% CI:−1.67to−0.21], reduced waiting time by 0.35 s [95% CI:−0.52to−0.14], and

modest effects, but they establish an important point: repeatedly choosing the exact instantaneous

4.2.2 Limitations of the QAOA Contribution
The strongest limitation of QAOA is visible in the same exact-optimum analysis. As the network
grows from four to nine intersections, QAOA becomes substantially less accurate with respect to
the Hamiltonian: recovery decreases from approximately 99% to approximately 40–45% across
much of the tested range, while optimality gaps increase by orders of magnitude in absolute
terms. The larger traffic effects observed on the3×3grid therefore must not be described as
evidence that QAOA becomes a better optimizer as network complexity increases. The opposite
occursmathematically: theoptimizationtaskbecomesharderwhiletheconnectionbetweenenergy

5 Conclusion
Reframing intersection timing as a pressure-based energy minimization problem, and extending
that formulation into a QAOA-based global optimization system, produces conditional rather than
universal improvements to traffic signal control. The results showed that no single controller dom-
inated across every network size, traffic regime, and performance metric. Pressure-Based Energy
Optimizationwasmostcompetitivewhendemandwassufficientlyhighandthenetworksufficiently
interconnected for switching stability, downstream traffic conditions, and neighboring-intersection
interactions to become important. Relative to Max-Pressure, the three energy-based controllers
generally became more competitive as the network expanded from four to nine intersections, par-
ticularlyinsaturatedthroughputandwaitingtimeandduringseveraldaytimeperiodsoftheSeattle
case study. At the same time, Max-Pressure remained particularly effective when demand was
sparseorthecorrectresponsewasdominatedbyanimmediatelyvisiblepressureimbalance. CoL-
ight and MPLight produced several of the strongest delay results. SCOOT demonstrated the value
of longer cycle times and long-term optimization in the 24-hour experiment. QAOA Optimization

to Classical Global by 1.68 s and 1.38 s on the2×2network and by 1.49 s and 1.78 s on the
3×3network. ThisshowsthatwhileQAOAOptimization’sadvantagesmightonlybeconditional,
the use of a quantum annealer still has benefits in certain scenarios. These advantages were not
universal, however, and the3×3,𝛼=0.5condition demonstrated an important tradeoff: QAOA

problem, the scale of the network, and whether the objective is network discharge, average travel
time, or stopped delay, with QAOA Optimization typically performing best to increase network

The study makes two main contributions. The first contribution is thePressure-Based Energy
Optimizationframework,whichextendsconventionalpressure-basedtrafficcontrolbyconverting
signal selection into an Ising-style energy minimization problem. The proposed objective com-

interactions with the surrounding network. The second contribution is theQAOA Optimization
