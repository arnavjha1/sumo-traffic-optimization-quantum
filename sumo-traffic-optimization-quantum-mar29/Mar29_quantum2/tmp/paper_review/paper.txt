1 Introduction
Urban traffic congestion is one of the most persistent and costly challenges facing modern cities.
In the United States, the 2025 Urban Mobility Report had estimated that the average driver lost
63 hours to traffic congestion in 2024, with nationwide congestion costs reaching approximately
$269 billion annually (1). Beyond economic damage, inefficient traffic flow elevates greenhouse
gas emissions and lowers air quality for urban residents. Cities and towns together now house
81% of the world’s 8.2 billion people, with two-thirds of future population growth projected to
occurincitiesthrough2050(2). Asglobalurbanizationcontinuestointensify,theneedtodevelop
smarter traffic management has never been greater. At the heart of this problem lies signalized
intersections, which are major sources of delay and capacity constraint in urban road networks,

where signal timing directly influences vehicle delay (3).
Forthepurposesofthisstudy,threeprimaryfailuremodeshadbeenconsidered:starvation,where
high-demandapproachesreceiveinsufficientgreentime;overflow,wheredownstreamqueuesspill
backintoupstreamapproachesandwasteavailablegreentime;andcongestion,wherelongqueues
and repeated acceleration delays reduce vehicle discharge efficiency through the network.
Traditionalfixed-timesignalsystemsoperateonpredeterminedcyclelengthsderivedfromhistorical
patterns and remain widely used for their simplicity and predictability. However, the fundamental
limitation of fixed-time systems is that they use predetermined timing plans and therefore cannot
react directly to unanticipated real-time demand fluctuations. Although coordinated fixed-time
systems can synchronize offsets across intersections, their timing plans remain pre-established
rather than continuously optimized from observed traffic conditions. Adaptive traffic control
systems improve upon fixed-time signals by adjusting phase timing in response to real-time traffic
conditions such as queue lengths. Some systems use local vehicle detectors to modify signal
timing at individual intersections, while coordinated systems such as SCOOT coordinate multiple
intersections along major roadways to improve traffic flow across a larger network (4). Max-
pressure control had used queue lengths to select signal phases that minimize the imbalance
betweenupstreamanddownstreamvehiclequeuesateachintersection(3). Reinforcementlearning
approaches treat signal control as a sequential decision-making problem, enabling intersections to
learn signal timing strategies that reduce congestion through repeated interaction with the traffic
environment (5). These adaptive approaches substantially expand beyond static timing, but they
differ in the scope and mechanism of coordination they provide. Max-Pressure, PressLight, and
MPLight primarily generate decisions from local or immediately downstream traffic conditions,
whereas systems such as SCOOT coordinate timing across multiple intersections and CoLight
explicitlyexchangeslearnedinformationbetweenneighboringsignals(4,6–8). Evenincoordinated
methods, however, the complete set of network-wide binary phase assignments is not generally
evaluated as a single shared optimization problem at every decision.
Achieving efficient traffic flow at the network level had remained challenging, however. Neigh-
boring intersections are inherently coupled since vehicles outflow from one intersection to all
neighboring ones. This means that it is important to also consider downstream queues as well as
upstream queues but also means that signal timing decisions cannot be optimized at the local level
without potentially sacrificing the performance of the overall network. For the binary two-phase
representation used in this study, simultaneous optimization of𝑁intersections had required con-
sideration of up to2𝑁 network-wide phase configurations. Exhaustive enumeration is therefore
practical for the2×2and3×3networks studied here (16 and 512 configurations, respectively),
but its cost had grown exponentially with network size and becomes increasingly unattractive for
larger networks (9). Pressure-based traffic control had provided a natural basis for such a global
formulation because queue imbalance can be represented as a cost associated with different sig-
nal configurations. In the proposed framework, these traffic-state costs had been encoded in an
Ising-style Hamiltonian containing pressure bias, downstream-congestion information, switching
penalties, and inter-intersection coupling. Inoue et al. formalize this approach through mod-
eling a50×50intersection lattice with binary signal states and demonstrating that minimizing
network-wide flow imbalance is equivalent to minimizing an Ising energy function (10). Solving

2.1.1 Experimental Design
Experiment 1: Saturated𝛼-Sweep
The first experiment had used 600-second saturated simulations to isolate the effect of vehicle
turning behavior on controller performance and measure how well controllers can perform under
extreme congestion in peak traffic conditions that would be present in urban cities such as Seattle.
The primary source of congestion and delay is from the peak hours of traffic rather than low
traffic conditions, so it is important to isolate each controller’s ability to adapt to these high traffic
conditions. Vehicle inflow had been held at the maximum feasible injection rate, simulating the
highest level of traffic congestion for a given network topology. The probability that a vehicle
continues straight through an intersection,𝛼, had been swept from 0.0 to 1.0 in increments of
0.1. The remaining probability,1−𝛼, is split equally between left and right turns. The saturated
𝛼-sweep had been performed on both the2×2and3×3networks to evaluate whether controller
behavior had remained consistent as network size and coordination complexity increase, since a
controller that had performed well on a limited network topology might not actually perform well
on an urban city network topology, which is much larger and complex.

4.1 Contribution 1: Pressure-Based Energy Optimization
4.1.1 Where Pressure-Based Energy Optimization Adds Value
The first contribution of this study is the Pressure-Based Energy Optimization framework. The
central question for this contribution is not whether Classical Local, Classical Global, or QAOA is
71

the strongest implementation of the energy objective; rather, it is whether adding the structure of
the energy formulation had provided useful traffic-control behavior beyond conventional pressure-
based control, which is used in controllers such as Max-Pressure. For this reason, Max-Pressure
had provided the most important conceptual benchmark. Both approaches respond to upstream
and downstream traffic conditions, but the proposed energy framework additionally had incor-
porated phase-switching cost and neighboring-intersection coupling into the decision objective.
The three energy-based controllers had therefore been considered collectively in this section as
implementations of the same underlying contribution.
The clearest evidence for the value of this additional structure appears when traffic demand is high
and interactions between intersections become important. In the saturated2×2experiment, the
energycontrollersweregenerallycompetitivewithMax-Pressureacrosstheintermediateandhigher
portionsofthe𝛼-sweep,particularlyinthroughput. At𝛼=0.7,forexample,thethreeenergy-based
controllers completed 941, 928, and 942 vehicles, compared with 906 under Max-Pressure. Their
waiting times were also comparable to Max-Pressure, remaining within approximately 0.4 s of its
39.11 s value. These results indicate that once traffic is sufficiently dense for network-wide signal
decisions to affect more than the immediately visible queue, the additional structure of the energy
objective can provide additional benefit to network discharge without a corresponding increase in
stopped delay.
This distinction becomes stronger on the3×3network, showing that the energy optimization
is more specialized for higher complexity scenarios on more complex network topologies. At

This scaling pattern had provided the strongest support for the proposed contribution. Max-

imbalance between an upstream queue and its downstream receiving link, but it is much more

benefit. The energy formulation had retained pressure information but also penalizes unnecessary
switching and had represented interactions between neighboring intersections. Those additional

4.1.2 Limitations of Pressure-Based Energy Optimization
The same comparisonwith Max-Pressure also haddefined the principal limitation ofthe contribu-
tion. When traffic is sparse and the correct signal action is largely determined by an immediately
visible queue, the additional energy terms can become unnecessary constraints rather than useful
coordination mechanisms. This is clearest during the nighttime portion of the2×2Seattle case
study. Max-Pressure achieved a night travel time of 41.36 s and waiting time of 10.48 s, whereas
all three energy-based controllers produced substantially larger delay. In such conditions, there is
little benefit in preserving phase stability or coordinating neighboring intersections that contain
limited traffic; quickly serving the active local queue can be more effective.
The particularly large difference in waiting time had suggested that this limitation is primarily
relatedtosignalresponsivenessratherthanvehiclemotionafterrelease. Thebiggestcontributorto
this delay is the lambda switching penalty. This switching penalty was implemented to discourage
switching signals becaues there is a typically unaccounted for cost of switching the traffic signal
to a different phase which is the yellow and all-red time that had been required to give vehicles
enough time to clear the middle of the intersection. This is almost 5 seconds of wasted time that
73

could otherwise be used to clear vehicles, so implementing this switching penalty really helps in
high traffic settings where even a single second wasted contributes to increased travel time and
increased waiting time with lower overall throughput. Under low traffic conditions, this simply
makestheenergy-basedoptimizationcontrollersslowertoreacttosuddenhadchangedindemand,
for example a singular car trying to make it through the grid at 3AM. In these scenarios, the
most optimal controllers will locally react to the change in demand and respond as quickly as
possible, not have to properly allocate every second possible to clear the most vehicles. This
slowerreactiontoachangeindemandharmstheenergyoptimizationcontrollersandMax-Pressure
significantly had outperformed them. The other contributor to this delay is the coupling term.
In a high-traffic scenario, it is important to ensure that vehicles can go through reliable platoon
progression, meaning a large group of vehicles is cleared at once through a line of green phases.
These platoons result in cars that have almost zero waiting time and very minimal travel time
while heavily increasing throughput in the short time these vehicles are present in the grid, which
contributes to more optimal performance in congested scenarios, which this coupling term really
excelsin. Theproblemisthatinlow-trafficscenarios,themostoptimalcontrollersaretheonesthat

4.2.1 What QAOA Adds Beyond Exact Classical Global Optimization

resulting vehicle volumes should not be interpreted as literal Seattle network totals.
Taken together, these assumptions had defined the scope of the conclusions in this study. The
experiments had been designed primarily to compare controller behavior under controlled, repeat-
abletrafficconditionsandtoisolatethecontributionsofpressure-basedenergyformulation,global
coordination, and QAOA search. They had not established field-deployment performance, real-
quantum-hardware advantage, or universal controller rankings across arbitrary urban networks.
Within the tested assumptions, however, all controllers had been evaluated under common traffic,
topology, vehicle-dynamics, and metric definitions, allowing their relative strengths, limitations,
and sensitivity to network complexity to be compared consistently.
